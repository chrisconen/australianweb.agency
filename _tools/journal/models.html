A free online store can be a sensible way to start selling. The useful question is what the word “free” covers: a limited plan, a short trial, access to software or an arrangement that earns money from each sale. Understanding the model makes it much easier to compare offers and plan your first year.

The right model depends on your catalogue and the people running it. A small business selling a few products has different requirements from a supplier managing trade accounts, complex variants and several warehouses. Start with your actual workflow rather than the length of a feature list.

A free trial is an evaluation period

A trial gives you time to explore an interface. It does not tell you the ongoing cost of a functioning store. Check the standard price after the trial, which features are included at that level and whether you can use your own domain.

Use real product information during the evaluation. Add a long product name, a variant, a delivery condition and the images you intend to publish. A polished empty template tells you less than one complete product moving through a realistic order.

For an ongoing free plan, ask how its limits are counted. One T-shirt in five sizes may be treated differently from five unrelated products. Find out what happens when you exceed a limit and whether the next plan changes the costs of the whole store.

No platform commission does not remove payment fees

The platform and the payment processor are different services. A platform may charge nothing on each order while your payment provider still charges for processing the transaction. Shipping, packaging and refunds can also affect the economics of a sale.

As a purely hypothetical example, a 2% platform commission on A$5,000 of eligible monthly sales would be A$100. That is an illustration of the calculation, not a statement about any named provider’s current pricing. Check whether a quoted percentage applies to product revenue, shipping, taxes or some other amount.

Six questions worth asking

Before approving any store project, get short written answers to these questions. They are more useful than a broad claim that a platform can “do everything”.

  1. Which products, variants and selling regions are included at launch?
  2. Who controls the domain and the merchant accounts?
  3. Which payment, delivery and accounting connections are included?
  4. Who handles updates, backups and incidents after launch?
  5. What data can be exported, in which format and at what cost?
  6. What changes if the catalogue, traffic or integration requirements grow?

Do not assume that software access automatically includes ongoing support or that hosting automatically includes every maintenance task. Define responsibility and response arrangements in the service agreement.
